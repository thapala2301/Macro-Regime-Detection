## Background and Concepts

### What is this project?

A quantitative macro research project investigating whether Hidden Markov Models can identify distinct macroeconomic regimes from publicly available data — and whether knowing the current regime improves asset allocation decisions compared to a static strategy.

**The core hypothesis:** Financial markets cycle through distinct regimes — risk-on growth, risk-off recession, inflationary stress, goldilocks stability. A strategy that works brilliantly in one regime can fail catastrophically in another. If we can detect which regime we are currently in, we can allocate to the assets that historically perform best in that regime.

---

### Why this matters to quant firms

Every major systematic macro fund — Brevan Howard, Man AHL, Marshall Wace, Aspect Capital — spends enormous research effort on regime detection. The 2022 inflationary shock exposed the failure of static allocation strategies: the traditional 60/40 portfolio lost roughly 16% because bonds, which are supposed to protect against equity drawdowns, fell simultaneously with stocks. A regime-aware model would have detected the inflationary environment and reallocated accordingly.

This is an active, unsolved problem in quantitative finance. No model perfectly predicts regime transitions. The question is whether a data-driven approach can do better than a static allocation.

---

### Why Hidden Markov Models?

Financial regimes are hidden — you cannot directly observe whether the market is in a risk-on or risk-off state. You can only observe the symptoms: what inflation is doing, where unemployment is, how steep the yield curve is, how wide credit spreads are. These observable symptoms are generated by an underlying hidden state — the regime.

Hidden Markov Models are designed exactly for this problem. They learn the hidden states from the observable data without being told what those states are. The model figures out the regimes from the patterns in the macro indicators. This is unsupervised learning applied to macroeconomics.

---

### Key concepts to understand

**Macroeconomic regime** — a persistent state of the economy characterised by a specific combination of growth, inflation, employment, and financial conditions. Examples: risk-on expansion (high growth, low inflation, tight spreads), inflationary stress (high inflation, rising rates, negative real returns), risk-off recession (rising unemployment, inverted yield curve, wide credit spreads).

**Yield curve slope** — the difference between long-term and short-term interest rates. 10-year Treasury yield minus 2-year Treasury yield. When positive, the economy is in a normal expansion — investors expect future growth. When negative (inverted), the bond market is pricing in a future recession. The yield curve has inverted before every US recession since 1960.

**Credit spread** — the extra yield investors demand to hold corporate bonds instead of risk-free government bonds. Wide spreads signal credit stress and economic fear. Narrow spreads signal confidence and risk appetite. One of the most reliable real-time indicators of financial conditions.

**Flight to safety** — during crises, investors sell risky assets (equities, commodities) and buy safe assets (US Treasuries, gold). This causes bond prices to rise sharply even as everything else falls. It is why TLT had its best month in November 2008 — at the peak of financial crisis panic, everyone was buying government bonds as a store of value.

**Hidden Markov Model (HMM)** — a statistical model with hidden states (regimes) that generate observable outputs (macro data). The model learns the most likely hidden states from the observed data using the Expectation-Maximisation algorithm. Once trained, the Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely sequence of hidden states across time.

### What we built

Connected to the FRED API (Federal Reserve Economic Data) and pulled six macroeconomic indicators across 20 years of monthly data. Simultaneously pulled monthly returns for four major asset classes from yfinance. Aligned everything to monthly frequency and combined into a single clean dataset.

**Macro indicators collected:**
- Fed funds rate — the Federal Reserve's main policy interest rate
- CPI inflation (year-on-year) — the rate of price increases in the economy
- Unemployment rate — the percentage of the labour force without work
- Yield curve slope (10Y minus 2Y Treasury) — recession predictor
- Credit spread (BAA minus 10Y Treasury) — financial stress indicator

**Asset classes collected:**
- SPY — S&P 500 equities
- TLT — 20-year US Treasury bonds
- GLD — Gold

### Correlation matrix analysis

| | Fed funds | Inflation | Unemployment | Yield curve | Credit spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Fed funds and unemployment (-0.60):** Strong negative correlation — exactly as macroeconomic theory predicts. When unemployment rises, consumer spending falls, GDP growth slows, and the central bank cuts interest rates to stimulate the economy. Lower rates reduce the cost of borrowing, encourage spending and investment, and inject money back into the circular flow of income. This is expansionary monetary policy in action.

**Yield curve and fed funds (-0.74):** The strongest correlation in the matrix. When the Fed raises rates aggressively (high fed funds), short-term rates rise faster than long-term rates, compressing or inverting the yield curve. This is exactly what happened in 2022-2023 — the fastest rate hiking cycle in 40 years flattened and inverted the yield curve simultaneously.

**Unemployment and yield curve (+0.74):** When unemployment is high (recession), the Fed cuts rates, short-term rates fall faster than long-term rates, and the yield curve steepens — moving from negative back to positive. This positive correlation captures the entire recessionary cycle.

**Credit spread and unemployment (+0.59):** When unemployment rises, corporate default risk increases, and investors demand a higher premium to hold corporate bonds. Credit spreads widen. Both move together in recessions and compress together in expansions.

**Inflation and everything (moderate negative):** Inflation is the most independent variable — it has moderate negative correlations with everything else rather than strong ones. This reflects that inflation can be driven by supply shocks (oil price, supply chain disruption) rather than just demand conditions. The 2022 inflation was primarily supply-driven, which is why it co-existed with tightening financial conditions rather than booming growth.

### Best and worst months by asset class

| Asset | Worst month | Date | Best month | Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPY | -16.52% | Oct 2008 | +12.70% | Apr 2020 |
| TLT | -13.07% | Jan 2009 | +14.34% | Nov 2008 |
| GLD | -16.14% | Oct 2008 | +12.79% | Nov 2009 |
| DJP | -21.52% | Oct 2008 | +13.84% | May 2009 |

**October 2008 — the worst month in modern financial history:**
SPY, GLD, and DJP all experienced their worst month simultaneously in October 2008. Lehman Brothers had collapsed in September 2008, triggering a global credit freeze. Banks stopped lending to each other overnight. Companies could not access credit markets. The entire financial system was on the verge of collapse. In that environment, investors were forced to sell everything liquid to raise cash — including gold, which is normally a safe haven. Forced liquidation overwhelmed every market simultaneously.

**November 2008 — TLT's best month ever (flight to safety):**
Just one month after the worst crash in modern history, US Treasury bonds had their best ever month. This is the flight to safety in action — investors fleeing from every risky asset and parking capital in US government bonds, the one instrument backed by the full faith of the US government which cannot default because it controls its own currency. The flood of buying pushed Treasury prices to record highs.

**January 2009 — TLT's worst month:**
Two months after its best month, TLT had its worst month. Why? Because by January 2009 the immediate panic had subsided. Investors began to believe the government response — TARP, Fed bailouts — would prevent a depression. Confidence returned, and money started flowing back out of Treasuries into riskier assets. This whipsaw from best to worst in two months captures how violently sentiment shifted during the crisis.

**April 2020 — SPY's best month ever:**
COVID crashed markets 34% in 33 days — the fastest crash in history. Then the Federal Reserve cut rates to zero overnight and the US government announced $2.2 trillion in stimulus. Markets immediately started pricing in a recovery that hadn't happened yet. April 2020 was the largest single monthly gain in SPY's history — not because the economy was recovering, but because investors believed the policy response was large enough to prevent a depression. Markets price the future, not the present.

### The 16 months where every asset fell simultaneously

In a normally functioning market, assets provide diversification — when stocks fall, bonds rise (flight to safety), gold rises (store of value). But in 16 of 236 months, every single asset class fell simultaneously. This is the most important finding of Day 1.

**Why this matters for the project:**
These 16 months represent regime failures — periods where normal asset class relationships broke down entirely. A static 60/40 portfolio has no defence against these periods. A regime-aware model that detected the underlying macro conditions and allocated accordingly would have been better positioned.

**The 2022-2023 cluster is the most striking:**
Six of the 16 months occurred in 2022-2023. This was the inflationary shock — the Federal Reserve raised rates from 0% to 5.33% in the fastest hiking cycle in 40 years. Rising rates hurt both stocks (higher discount rates reduce equity valuations) and bonds (existing bonds lose value when new bonds offer higher yields) simultaneously. The traditional negative correlation between stocks and bonds — the entire foundation of 60/40 investing — collapsed. Diversification failed exactly when investors needed it most.

The 2022 inflationary regime is the clearest example of why regime detection matters. It was a fundamentally different market environment that required a fundamentally different allocation strategy. The macro indicators — inflation at 8.98%, fed funds rising rapidly, yield curve inverting — were all signalling this regime loudly. A model that detected it could have allocated to commodities or cash rather than holding stocks and bonds simultaneously through their worst joint drawdown in decades.

### What the macro indicator chart showed

Plotting all five macro indicators from 2006 to 2026 revealed something powerful — you can already see the distinct regimes with your own eyes before the model touches the data.

**2008-2009 — the financial crisis regime:**
Fed funds crashes from 5.25% to near zero. Credit spreads spike to 6% — the highest reading in the entire 20-year dataset. Unemployment surges from 4.5% to nearly 10%. The yield curve goes from slightly negative to steeply positive as the Fed cuts short-term rates aggressively. Inflation briefly goes negative — the red shaded area below zero — the only deflationary period in the dataset. Every single indicator screaming recession simultaneously. This is the clearest regime in the data — impossible to miss visually.

**2010-2019 — the long goldilocks regime:**
Fed funds near zero for years. Inflation stable around 2%. Unemployment slowly grinding down from 10% to 3.5% over nearly a decade. Yield curve consistently positive and stable. Credit spreads compressed and flat at historically low levels. This is the goldilocks regime — low rates, low inflation, slow steady growth. The longest bull market in modern history. Almost every macro indicator is calm and trending in a benign direction for nearly a decade.

**2020 — the COVID shock:**
Unemployment spikes to 14.8% — the sharpest spike in the entire chart, almost a vertical line, then crashes back down just as fast. Nothing else in 20 years of data looks remotely like this. Fed funds drops to zero overnight. The most extreme single event in the dataset compressed into 2-3 months. Then an almost immediate return to near pre-crisis conditions as the policy response took effect.

**2022-2023 — the inflationary regime:**
Fed funds rockets from zero to 5.33% in the fastest hiking cycle visible anywhere in the chart. Inflation peaks at 8.98% — levels not seen since the 1980s. The yield curve inverts — the red shaded area below zero in the green chart — as short-term rates exceed long-term rates. Credit spreads widen. This regime is fundamentally different from anything in the preceding decade. The goldilocks assumptions of low rates and low inflation that drove the 2010-2019 bull market were completely reversed.

**What the chart tells us about regime detection:**

Even before running a single model, the chart reveals approximately four distinct macro regimes across 20 years:

1. **Pre-crisis 2006-2007** — high rates, normal conditions, moderately positive yield curve
2. **Crisis and recovery 2008-2015** — zero rates, high unemployment, steeply positive yield curve, elevated credit spreads
3. **Goldilocks 2015-2021** — low rates, low inflation, falling unemployment, compressed spreads
4. **Inflationary 2022-2023** — surging rates, high inflation, inverted yield curve, widening spreads

The Hidden Markov Model should identify these same regimes from the data without being told they exist. If it does, that validates both the model and our choice of macro indicators. If it finds different regimes, that is equally interesting — it may be detecting patterns that are not immediately visible to the human eye.
