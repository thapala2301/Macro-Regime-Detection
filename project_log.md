## Background and Concepts

### What is this project?

A quantitative macro research project investigating whether Hidden Markov Models can identify distinct macroeconomic regimes from publicly available data — and whether knowing the current regime improves asset allocation decisions compared to a static strategy.

**The core hypothesis:** Financial markets cycle through distinct regimes — risk-on growth, risk-off recession, inflationary stress, goldilocks stability. A strategy that works brilliantly in one regime can fail catastrophically in another. If we can detect which regime we are currently in, we can allocate to the assets that historically perform best in that regime.

---

### Why this matters to quant firms

Every major systematic macro fund — Brevan Howard, Man AHL, Marshall Wace, Aspect Capital — spends enormous research effort on regime detection. The 2022 inflationary shock exposed the failure of static allocation strategies: the traditional 60/40 portfolio lost roughly 16% because bonds, which are supposed to protect against equity drawdowns, fell simultaneously with stocks. A regime-aware model would have detected the inflationary environment and reallocated accordingly.

---

### Why Hidden Markov Models?

Financial regimes are hidden — you cannot directly observe whether the market is in a risk-on or risk-off state. You can only observe the symptoms: what inflation is doing, where unemployment is, how steep the yield curve is, how wide credit spreads are. These observable symptoms are generated by an underlying hidden state — the regime.

Hidden Markov Models are designed exactly for this problem. They learn the hidden states from the observable data without being told what those states are.

---

### Key concepts

**Macroeconomic regime** — a persistent state of the economy characterised by a specific combination of growth, inflation, employment, and financial conditions.

**Yield curve slope** — the difference between long-term and short-term interest rates. When negative (inverted), the bond market is pricing in a future recession. The yield curve has inverted before every US recession since 1960.

**Credit spread** — the extra yield investors demand to hold corporate bonds instead of risk-free government bonds. Wide spreads signal credit stress and economic fear.

**Flight to safety** — during crises, investors sell risky assets and buy US Treasuries and gold. This is why TLT had its best month in November 2008 — at the peak of financial crisis panic, everyone was buying government bonds.

**Hidden Markov Model (HMM)** — a statistical model with hidden states (regimes) that generate observable outputs (macro data). The model learns the most likely hidden states from the observed data using the Expectation-Maximisation algorithm. Once trained, the Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely sequence of hidden states across time.

## Day 2 — HMM Theory: Understanding the Model Before Touching Code
**Date:** August 2026
**No code — pure conceptual understanding**

### Why do we need an HMM at all?

Looking back at the macro indicator chart from Day 1, you could already see four regimes visually. So the obvious question is: why not just label them manually?

Three reasons:

**Subjectivity** — if you label regimes manually, someone else would draw the boundaries differently. A model finds them objectively from the data with no human bias.

**Real-time detection** — in practice you do not know you are in a new regime until you are already in it. A model assigns a probability to the current regime in real time as new data arrives. Manual labelling only works in hindsight.

**Uncertainty** — a model does not just say "you are in regime 2." It says "you are 87% likely in regime 2 and 13% likely in regime 3." That uncertainty is useful — it tells you how confident to be in your allocation decision.

---

### The three components of a Hidden Markov Model

#### Component 1 — Hidden states

Symptoms are things like high unemployment and stunted growth. A regime is like a recession — it cannot be directly observed. You cannot measure it directly. You can only measure its symptoms — unemployment at 9%, credit spreads at 5%, yield curve inverted, inflation at 8%. From those symptoms you infer the hidden state. That is the hidden part of Hidden Markov Model.

#### Component 2 — The Markov property

The next regime depends only on the current state. If you are in a recession this month, the probability of being in recovery next month depends only on the fact that you are currently in recession. It does not matter whether the recession was caused by a financial crisis, a pandemic, or an oil shock. The entire history of the economy is irrelevant — only where you are now matters.

#### Component 3 — The transition matrix

A matrix of probabilities defining the odds of moving to one state when you are currently in another. It is a 4x4 table — current regime on one axis, next regime on the other. The diagonal entries tend to be high because regimes are persistent — once you are in a recession you tend to stay there for several months.

#### Component 4 — The emission distribution

Each regime has a characteristic fingerprint in the macro data. The emission distribution is a multivariate Gaussian — a bell curve extended to multiple dimensions simultaneously. For each regime it learns two things:
- **Mean vector** — the average values of all five macro indicators in this regime
- **Covariance matrix** — how much each indicator varies and how they move together

A multivariate Gaussian is needed rather than five separate normal distributions because it handles the relationships between all indicators simultaneously. The indicators are not independent — unemployment and credit spreads move together in a recession, yield curve and fed funds are strongly correlated. Five separate distributions would treat each indicator as if it had nothing to do with the others, which the correlation matrix from Day 1 already proved is wrong.

---

### The EM Algorithm — how the model learns

The learning problem is circular:
- To estimate the emission distributions you need to know which regime each month belongs to
- To know which regime each month belongs to you need the emission distributions

You do not know either to start with. The Expectation-Maximisation algorithm solves this by breaking the circular problem into two alternating steps:

**E-step (Expectation):** Given your current best guess at the parameters, calculate the probability of being in each regime at each point in time.

**M-step (Maximisation):** Given those regime probabilities, update the parameters — the transition matrix and emission distributions — to maximise the likelihood of observing the data you actually have.

Then repeat. E then M, E then M, until convergence.

This works even though you start knowing nothing about the regimes because it is an iterative process — like the cobweb model or Newton-Raphson methods. You spam iterations and it converges to a final answer eventually even if you start in a random place, provided you do not start somewhere that triggers divergence. Each iteration improves the likelihood, just like Newton-Raphson steps toward a root.

One important caveat: EM converges to a local maximum, not necessarily the global one. That is why in practice we run it multiple times from different random starting points and take the best result.

---

### The Viterbi Algorithm — how the model decodes regimes

Once the model is trained, you want to answer: what regime was the economy actually in each month across 20 years?

You might think: just pick the most likely regime at each timestamp independently. But that ignores the Markov property entirely. The Viterbi algorithm finds the single most likely sequence of regimes across all 236 months simultaneously, respecting the transition probabilities. It uses dynamic programming — working forward through time, at each step keeping track of the most likely path to reach each state, then tracing backwards at the end to find the optimal sequence.

We need Viterbi rather than independent regime assignment because we need to account for the Markov property. The current state is the only thing that influences the future state, and the diagonals of the transition matrix tend to be high because persisting in the same regime is more common than switching. If we relied purely on EM results with regimes fluctuating month by month, we would disobey the Markov property entirely. Viterbi finds the best balance between the most likely regime at each month and respecting the transition probabilities simultaneously.

---

### BIC — choosing the number of regimes

More regimes always fits the training data better — a 10-state model will find incredibly detailed patterns. But those patterns might just be noise, not genuine economic regimes. This is overfitting.

Without a penalty we would just spam as many regimes as possible to get fine-grained data, but in doing so we begin to blur the lines between detailed regimes and noise. The penalty puts a restraint and this becomes a game of optimisation — finding the best parameters under the lowest penalty.

**Formula: BIC = -2 × log(likelihood) + k × log(n)**

Where k is the number of parameters and n is the number of observations. We want to **minimise BIC** — best fit with the least complexity.

---

### Worked Example — HMM from scratch with two regimes

To make all of the above concrete, here is a simplified worked example built by hand.

**The setup:**

Two hidden regimes: Expansion and Recession. One observable indicator: unemployment rate. Five months of data:

| Month | Unemployment |
|---|---|
| 1 | 4.2% |
| 2 | 4.5% |
| 3 | 8.8% |
| 4 | 9.1% |
| 5 | 8.6% |

Transition matrix:
- Expansion → stay Expansion: 0.9, move to Recession: 0.1
- Recession → stay Recession: 0.8, move to Expansion: 0.2

Emission distributions:
- Expansion: Normal(mean=4%, std=0.5%)
- Recession: Normal(mean=9%, std=0.5%)

**Step 1 — Eyeballing the data:**

Months 1 and 2 lock in on expansion as the unemployment rates of 4.2% and 4.5% are very close to the expansion mean of 4%. Months 3-5 are centred more around recession with values of 8.8%, 9.1%, and 8.6% sitting close to the recession mean of 9%.

This is intuition doing what Viterbi does mathematically — finding the most likely sequence of hidden states given the observed data.

**Step 2 — Emission probabilities:**

For Month 1 (unemployment = 4.2%):
- P(observing 4.2% | Expansion) = HIGH — 4.2% is only 0.4 percentage points from the expansion mean
- P(observing 4.2% | Recession) = VERY LOW — 4.2% is 4.8 percentage points from the recession mean

Month 1 very clearly belongs to Regime 1 (Expansion) as the unemployment rate of 4.2% is very close to the expansion mean of 4% — hence justified.

The probability is computed from the Gaussian formula. The further a data point sits from a regime's mean in standard deviation terms, the lower its probability of belonging to that regime.

**Step 3 — Why Viterbi matters (the ambiguous case):**

Imagine Month 3 had unemployment of 6.5% — exactly between the two means. The emission probability alone cannot determine the regime clearly. This is where the transition matrix saves you.

Month 2 was almost certainly Expansion. The transition matrix says P(Recession | currently Expansion) = 0.1. So even with an ambiguous emission, the transition probability strongly pushes Month 3 toward staying in Expansion.

If instead the transition probability from Expansion to Recession was 0.9, the transition would suggest we move from expansion to recession as opposed to remaining in expansion — the transition matrix would override the ambiguity and push Month 3 toward Recession.

Viterbi combines both pieces of information — emission probability AND transition probability — to find the most likely assignment at each step.

**Step 4 — EM iteration:**

Imagine you did not know the means (4% and 9%) or the transition matrix. You only have the five unemployment observations. EM solves this:

*Iteration 1 — random starting guess:*
- Expansion mean = 5%, Recession mean = 7%
- Transition matrix: all 0.5 (equal probability)

*E-step:* Using those guesses, calculate probabilities.
- Month 1 (4.2%): closer to 5% than 7% → roughly 60% Expansion, 40% Recession
- Month 3 (8.8%): closer to 7% than 5% → roughly 70% Recession, 30% Expansion

Assignments are weak because the initial guess was poor.

*M-step:* Update means using probabilities as weights.
- New Expansion mean = weighted average of all months, weighted by their Expansion probability → shifts toward 4%
- New Recession mean = weighted average of all months, weighted by their Recession probability → shifts toward 9%

*Iteration 2:* Means are now closer to truth. Assignments sharpen — Month 1 is now 90% Expansion.

*Iteration 3:* Sharper still. Converges toward true means of 4% and 9%.

**Why assignments sharpen with each iteration:** More iterations of EM allow us to adjust parameters based on probabilities and incorporate them to find weighted averages for the means. The values will eventually tend towards a definitive value such as 4%. Each iteration moves the means closer to the true cluster centres — as the means improve, the probabilities sharpen, and as the probabilities sharpen, the means improve further. It is a self-reinforcing cycle that converges to a stable solution — the same intuition as Newton-Raphson where each step gets you closer and the steps get smaller until you stop moving.

---

### Summary — the full HMM pipeline

| Component | What it does |
|---|---|
| Hidden states | Regimes you cannot observe directly, only infer from symptoms |
| Markov property | Next state depends only on current state, not history |
| Transition matrix | 4x4 probabilities of moving between regimes, high diagonals |
| Emission distributions | Multivariate Gaussians capturing each regime's macro fingerprint |
| EM algorithm | Iterative E and M steps converging like Newton-Raphson |
| Viterbi algorithm | Dynamic programming to find most likely regime sequence |
| BIC | Minimise to find optimal number of regimes |

## Day 3 — First HMM Implementation and Regime Detection
**Date:** August 2026
**Script:** `hmm_learn.py`

### What we built

Implemented the first Hidden Markov Model on the macro dataset collected in Day 1. The pipeline covered four blocks: data standardisation, model fitting, regime decoding via Viterbi, and regime characterisation. The key question going in — would the model find the same four regimes we identified visually in Day 1 without being told anything about economic history?

### Block 1 — Data standardisation

Loaded `macro_data.csv` and selected the five macro features: fed funds rate, inflation, unemployment, yield curve slope, and credit spread. Applied `StandardScaler` from scikit-learn to standardise each feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

Standardisation is essential because the five indicators operate on completely different scales — unemployment ranges from 3-15%, credit spread from 1-6%, fed funds from 0-5.33%. Without standardisation the HMM would weight unemployment more heavily simply because its numbers are larger, not because it is more informative. Scaling to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 puts all five indicators on equal footing so the model judges them purely by their information content.

### Block 2 — Fitting the HMM

Fitted a `GaussianHMM` with the following parameters:
- `n_components=4` — four hidden regimes
- `covariance_type='full'` — full covariance matrix capturing relationships between all indicators
- `n_iter=1000` — up to 1000 EM iterations
- `random_state=42` — fixed seed for reproducibility

The model trained and converged successfully — `monitor_.converged = True` — meaning the EM algorithm found a stable solution before hitting the iteration limit.

**Initial issue:** The first run without `random_state` and with only 100 iterations produced unstable results — regimes flickering between states 0 and 3 every single month in certain periods, violating the Markov property. Increasing iterations to 1000 and fixing the random seed resolved this completely, producing stable persistent regimes as expected.

### Block 3 — Viterbi decoding

Called `model.predict(X)` to run the Viterbi algorithm and assign a regime label (0, 1, 2, or 3) to every one of the 236 monthly observations. Plotted the regime sequence over time alongside unemployment with colour-coded regime shading.

**The regime sequence produced:**
- Regime 2 — November 2006 to early 2008
- Brief transitions through regimes 0 and 1 — 2008 crisis onset
- Regime 3 — 2009 to approximately 2013
- Regime 1 — 2013 to 2020
- Regime 2 briefly — pre-COVID
- Regime 1 — COVID period
- Regime 3 briefly — early post-COVID
- Regime 0 — 2021 to 2023
- Regime 2 — 2023 to present

### Block 5 — Transition matrix

```
                    Inflationary Shock  Goldilocks  Pre-Crisis Normal  Crisis & Recovery
```

**The diagonal entries confirm regime persistence:**
- Pre-Crisis Normal: 97.1% probability of staying in the same regime month to month
- Crisis & Recovery: 96.4%
- Goldilocks: 94.5%
- Inflationary Shock: 93.2% — the least persistent, meaning inflationary shocks tend to resolve faster than other regimes

**The zero entries are economically meaningful:**
- Goldilocks never transitions directly to Inflationary Shock (0.000) — you cannot jump from low inflation to high inflation in a single month
- Crisis & Recovery never transitions directly to Pre-Crisis Normal (0.000) — you cannot jump from 8% unemployment to high rates without passing through a recovery period first
- These structural zeros validate that the model has learned genuine economic dynamics rather than statistical noise

### Key research finding — Day 3

The Hidden Markov Model successfully identified four distinct macroeconomic regimes from 20 years of FRED data without being told anything about economic history. The four regimes — Inflationary Shock, Goldilocks, Pre-Crisis Normal, and Crisis & Recovery — match the four periods identified visually in Day 1 and align precisely with known historical events: the 2008 financial crisis, the 2010-2019 recovery and bull market, the 2022 inflationary shock.

The transition matrix confirms high regime persistence (all diagonals above 0.93) and economically sensible transition restrictions (no direct jump from goldilocks to inflationary shock). The model has learned genuine macroeconomic structure from the data.
